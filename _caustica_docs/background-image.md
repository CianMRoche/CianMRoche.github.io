Two pictures can sit behind the lensed image, and they do different jobs. The **Background** (the Background panel; collapsed by default on phones) is decoration: a picture framed to the square panel, behind everything. The **Observation** (the Observation panel, the eye icon) is data: the observed image of a lens, placed on the sky at its pixel scale, which you line a model up against, which the **Delensed image** view (below) traces back to the source plane, and which masks cut. Neither is a physical object: they are not lensed, contribute nothing to any deflection, and appear in the plain lensed-image view only, since the quantity maps colour every pixel themselves and Line art paints its own opaque palette background. The flat **field colour** behind both is set in the **Field** row of the Colors section.

**Field color** is the flat colour of the empty field, and applies whether or not a picture is loaded (it also fills the margins a **Contain** fit leaves over, and shows through where a picture is hidden). It is stored as its dark-theme value and handed to the shader as-is, exactly like a source's own colour, so the light-theme canvas inversion flips it along with everything else; that is what keeps the default black meaning "the plain empty field", which the inversion turns white in light theme as it always has. The picker shows the complement in light theme, so what you see there is what appears on the plot.

Its main use is with a pasted-image source. The rectangular boundary of a pasted image is visible only because it is a step between the picture and the field, so matching the two makes it disappear. **Match image** does this in one click: it averages the colour in a ring a few pixels deep around the border of the pasted image (weighted by alpha, so transparent padding is skipped) and sets the field to it. A ring rather than the single outermost row, since photographs often carry a compression fringe or a stray dark line right at the boundary. The source's own **Edge blend** (<a href="/caustica-documentation/source-models/">§6</a>) then feathers whatever step is left. Both the field and the pasted image reach the screen through the same as-is path, so equal values match in either theme.

Both pictures are uploaded to the GPU as textures and sampled in the same fragment shader. The backdrop $I_\text{bg}$ is built up in layers, the field colour, then the background over it, then the observation over that (each weighted by its own coverage, below), and the whole is composited **underneath** the lensed light, using the source brightness itself as the backdrop's coverage:

$$I_\text{out} = \operatorname{clamp}\Big(I_\text{src}' + I_\text{bg}\,(1 - C),\ 0,\ 1\Big), \qquad C = \max\big(\max_c I_{\text{src},c}',\ A_\text{max}\big), \qquad I_\text{src}' = f(I_\text{src}),$$

where $I_\text{src}'$ is the stretched-and-recombined source light of <a href="/caustica-documentation/source-models/">§6</a>, with $f$ applied before the composite so it shapes only the lensed light and leaves a supplied picture's own tones intact, and $A_\text{max}$ is the largest pasted-image footprint at that pixel (<a href="/caustica-documentation/source-models/">§6</a>). Coverage $C$ is therefore brightness for the analytic sources, whose light genuinely fades to nothing, and footprint for a pasted image, which is opaque wherever it exists. A fully lit pixel hides the backdrop completely and an empty one shows it in full, so each picture's **Brightness** ($\beta \in [0,2]$, applied to its own layer) fades it between what lies behind it ($0$) and its supplied tones ($1$), or past them, and cannot alter the lensed image itself.

<div class="doc-note" markdown="1">
**Why not a plain sum?** Simply adding $\beta I_\text{bg}$ to every pixel is the physically natural composite (light behind the field adds to light from the source), but it makes the Brightness slider shift *every* pixel by the backdrop's value, and where the sum saturates it also un-clips arcs as the backdrop is dimmed, so the lensed image visibly changes with a control that is supposed to affect only the backdrop. Weighting by $1 - \max_c I'_{\text{src},c}$ keeps the source's own contribution untouched at every brightness setting; the backdrop still shows through partially lit pixels, as anything behind a semi-transparent layer must.
</div>

<div class="doc-note" markdown="1">
**Light theme.** The image canvas is CSS-inverted in light theme so the lensed light reads dark on white, which would show a photograph as a negative. The shader therefore **pre-inverts** both pictures, $I \to 1 - I$, cancelling the canvas inversion: they look the same in either theme while every other plot element (arcs, overlay ink, source colours) still flips with the theme exactly as it did before. The inversion is applied before the brightness scale, so a Brightness of $0$ is the plain empty field in both themes (black in dark, white in light) rather than black in one and white in the other. PNG capture and video recording replicate the same inversion, so exports match the screen.
</div>

**Edge blend** ($0$ to $1$, on each picture) fades a picture into what lies behind it over a band inside its border, so its rectangle stops showing as a hard step: the picture's weight is $f(d_x)\,f(d_y)$, with $d$ the distance inside the nearer edge on each axis and $f$ a smoothstep from $0$ at the edge to $1$ at a width of $0.4$ times the blend times the picture's shorter side (the reach of a pasted image's Edge blend, <a href="/caustica-documentation/source-models/">§6</a>), so the band is the same angle on both axes.

## Background

**Framing.** The image panel is always square, so a rectangular picture has to be framed. **Fit** picks the starting frame, then **Zoom** and **Offset X / Y** adjust it by hand. Writing $a = w/h$ for the image's aspect ratio, the fit sets how much of the texture the viewport spans on each axis:

| Fit | Span $(s_x, s_y)$ | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cover (crop) | $\big(\min(1, 1/a),\ \min(1, a)\big)$ | Fills the square; the long axis is cropped. Default. |
| Contain (fit) | $\big(\max(1, 1/a),\ \max(1, a)\big)$ | Whole image visible; the remainder stays empty field. |
| Stretch | $(1, 1)$ | Exactly one image on each axis; distorts a non-square picture. |
| Tile (mirrored) | as Contain, wrapped | Whole image visible, mirror-repeated to fill the rest. |

**Zoom** $k$ divides the span, so a larger zoom shows less texture and magnifies the picture; the **offsets** pan by a fraction of the panel (a positive offset moves the image right or up). Sampling outside $[0,1]^2$, which a Contain fit or a zoom below the fit produces, falls back to the field colour rather than smearing the edge pixels, except under Tile, where the texture's mirrored wrap fills the plane instead (Edge blend does nothing there, as Tile has no edge). The screen-space sample point is therefore

$$\mathbf{uv} = \Big(\mathbf{v} - \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}\Big) \odot \frac{\mathbf{s}}{k} \;+\; \frac{1}{2},$$

with $\mathbf{v}$ the viewport UV, $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ the two offsets, and $\odot$ elementwise multiplication; the vertical component is then flipped, since a texture's first data row is the image's top.

**Anchoring.** By default the backdrop is fixed to the viewport: it fills the panel whatever the field of view, so zooming the lens does not zoom the sky behind it. **Zoom with FOV** anchors it to the sky instead, scaling the span by $\text{fov}/\text{fov}_0$ where $\text{fov}_0$ is the field of view frozen when the image was loaded (or when the option was switched on). The backdrop then magnifies along with the lensed image, at the cost of running out of pixels when zoomed far in. A picture that belongs at a definite place on the sky is an observation (below), not a background.

An **eye button** beside the upload and remove buttons takes the backdrop off the plot without unloading it, so a figure can be captured with and without it. **Reset framing** returns Fit, Zoom, the offsets, Brightness, Edge blend and the anchoring to their defaults, and shows the picture if it was hidden.

The framing round-trips through the config file (`bgFit`, `bgZoom`, `bgOffX`, `bgOffY`, `bgBright`, `bgEdgeBlend`, `bgSkyLock`, `bgHidden`), each validated on load: `bgFit` against the allowlist and the rest clamped to the ranges above, so a hand-edited file cannot push them out of bounds, and a file predating the feature simply falls back to the defaults. Images larger than 4096 px on their longest side are downscaled on load (for the observation too), since a picture is never displayed above canvas resolution.

A picture you upload yourself is not saved in the config, exactly as for pasted-image sources (which have their own `image:` key for site-shipped pictures, <a href="/caustica-documentation/source-models/">§6</a>); nor is the sky-lock reference $\text{fov}_0$, which is re-frozen at the current field of view whenever an image loads. A config *can* name one of the pictures shipped alongside the presets, through the optional `bgImage` key. Loading a config with no `bgImage` drops a preset-supplied image but leaves a hand-uploaded one alone, so switching presets neither discards your own picture nor leaves the previous preset's behind. The observation's `obsImage` key (below) follows the same rules.

## Observation

The **Observation** panel holds the observed picture, its masks, and the way into the delensed view. An observation is placed on the sky by its **pixel scale** $p$ (arcseconds per pixel of the image as supplied, before any downscale on load), spanning $W p \times H p$ arcsec for a $W \times H$ pixel image, with its centre at **Offset X / Y**, in arcseconds ($+$ right and up). The viewport then covers $\text{fov}/(Wp)$ and $\text{fov}/(Hp)$ of the texture on the two axes whatever the zoom, so the picture stays put on the sky as the field of view changes, and the lens model you line up against its arcs is exactly the one the Delensed view traces through. The offsets have to be angles rather than fractions of the panel for the same reason. A new picture starts with the whole of it fitted to the current field of view, $p = \text{fov}/\max(W, H)$, centred; the line under the **Pixel scale** box gives its size on the sky, and the offset sliders reach up to half its longer side from the axis.

**Show in the lensed view** draws the observation over the background, behind the lensed light, in the lensed-image view (on by default); the Delensed view reads it whether shown or not. **Brightness** and **Edge blend** apply in both views (behind the lensed image the blend fades it into the background or the field). **Remove** takes the picture away; its masks stay, at their places on the sky, ready for the next one.

**Use lensed image** takes the lensed image as it is now as the observation. It is drawn afresh in the lensed-image view under the settings of a tab showing that view (the instrument model included, if one is on), with no backdrop and a black field, and only the rendered frame is kept, so no overlay, annotation or legend comes with it. Its pixel scale is set to the field of view over the frame's width in pixels, centred, so it lies on the sky exactly where the light was, at the render resolution (Settings, render scale). It starts unshown, since behind the lensed image it would only double the light, with Brightness 1. Delensing it through the same lens model rebuilds the sources (up to resolution, the $\lvert\mu\rvert$ cut, and light that never reached the frame), which makes it a check of the delensing and a way to see a model's own image through it. Like an uploaded picture it is not saved in a config.

**In the scene drawer.** The observation has its own card at the left end of the drawer, the observer's end of the line of sight (z = 0), with a dashed viewer showing the picture where it sits on the sky and its masks over it, in the same field as the plane viewers. The strip's left end is drawn as the observer's eye. Clicking the card, or the eye, opens the Observation panel and outlines the card; pointing at either lights the other.

The observation round-trips through the config file (`obsImage`, `obsPixel`, `obsOffX`, `obsOffY`, `obsBright`, `obsEdge`, `obsShown`), clamped on load like the background's keys, with its masks in a top-level `masks:` list (below). The **Image recreation** preset (<a href="/caustica-documentation/presets/">§11</a>) ships its quasar photograph this way, at $p = 0.003125$″, so it is in place and delenses as soon as the preset loads. Configs from before the observation existed delensed the background picture: one that gave it a pixel scale (`bgPixel` $\gt 0$) is read as an observation, its picture, scale, sky offsets, visibility, brightness and blend moving over. Masks kept on planes, as older configs did, move to the observation, keeping their places on the sky.

## Delensed view

The **Delensed image** view (key `U`) runs the lens backwards on the observation. Each of its pixels is traced through the current lens model to the source plane at the reference $z_s$ (the **z<sub>s</sub> ref** of <a href="/caustica-documentation/lensing-quantities/">§4</a>), and its colour is painted where its light came from. With the right lens model the multiple images of a source fall on top of each other and reassemble into one source; with a wrong one they land as offset copies, which is what makes the view useful for fitting by eye. Lenses can be dragged and reshaped while watching the copies merge. The Observation panel's **Open the delensed view** button opens it beside the current view (or goes to its tab if one is open).

**Method.** A per-pixel inversion would have to solve the lens equation for every source-plane pixel, and a per-pixel scatter of image pixels to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ leaves holes wherever the magnification is high. Instead the image-plane field of the observation, wherever its offsets place it on the sky, is covered by a grid of $512$ nodes along its long axis (fewer along the short one, in proportion), drawn as a triangle mesh whose vertices sit at their traced $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and whose texture coordinates are their place in the picture. Rasterising the mesh lays the picture into the source plane with the triangles stretching to fill the gaps, and inside the caustics, where the mesh folds over itself, several sheets land on the same pixel and are combined:

$$I(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\sum_i \lvert\mu_i\rvert\, I_\text{obs}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_i)}{\sum_i \lvert\mu_i\rvert},$$

the sum running over the images $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$ of $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ inside the picture. Weighting by $\lvert\mu\rvert$ makes this the average over all image pixels that land on a source pixel, which is what a scatter would compute: a more magnified image samples the source more finely, so it contributes more pixels. Surface brightness is conserved by lensing, so for a correct model every term agrees and the weights only decide how disagreements are settled.

<div class="doc-note" markdown="1">
**Implementation.** A first pass reuses the main fragment shader's multiplane tracer to write, for every grid node, $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, $\lvert\mu\rvert$, and the short axis of the image patch a unit of the source plane maps onto ($1/\sigma_\text{max}$ of the Jacobian, by central differences with a step of a quarter node) into a float texture. The mesh pass draws two triangles per grid cell with no vertex buffer at all (each vertex reads its node from that texture by `gl_VertexID`) and accumulates $\lvert\mu\rvert I$ and $\lvert\mu\rvert$ into a half-float target by additive blending; a resolve pass divides them out. Both targets need WebGL2 float render targets (`EXT_color_buffer_float`, available almost everywhere), and the Observation panel says so on a GPU without them. The magnification is interpolated from the nodes rather than taken from each triangle's screen-space derivatives, because the cells are often smaller than a pixel, most of all on a magnified arc, where the rasteriser's sub-pixel vertex snapping makes those derivatives noisy by tens of percent. The texture's mip level is set explicitly for the same reason, from the patch's short axis: along an arc a source pixel covers a long thin strip of the image, every pixel of which comes from that same source pixel, so only the width across the arc decides how much detail is averaged away.
</div>

Two thresholds keep the mesh honest. Triangles with $\lvert\mu\rvert \lt 0.05$ are dropped: these are the most demagnified regions, chiefly around a singular lens centre, which map a handful of image pixels across a wide patch of the source plane. Their weight is already small, but where nothing else covers a source pixel they would fill it with a smear of whatever sits at the lens centre. The weight is capped at $\lvert\mu\rvert = 100$ so the thin, nearly degenerate triangles straddling a critical curve cannot swamp a pixel. Source-plane pixels that no part of the picture reaches show the empty field.

As a check, a Gaussian source rendered through an SIE and fed back in as the observation is recovered to about $0.1\%$ rms of its peak, its centre within a pixel; the residual sits along the radial caustic, where the poorly resolved central image enters or leaves the average.

**Display.** The view is a source-plane map, like the image-number view (<a href="/caustica-documentation/lensing-quantities/">§4</a>), so the overlay drops the image-plane features (critical curves, point-source image dots and their time delays, stretch whiskers) and keeps the caustics, lens and mask markers, and ruler. Unlike the image-number map it keeps the sources as well, since a source marker sits at its own source-plane position and can be laid over its reconstruction. The picture's own tones are shown in either theme, scaled by the observation's **Brightness** and clipped at white, and faded into the empty field along its edges by its **Edge blend** (both applied to each image pixel before the averaging, so they change neither the weights nor which source-plane pixels are reached). With no observation the image says where to add one.

**Masks.** A mask cuts an elliptical patch out of the observation before it is delensed, typically over the lens galaxy, whose own light would otherwise be traced back with everything else and spread over the source plane (mostly around the radial caustic), or over a foreground star. Masked pixels contribute nothing: a source-plane point whose images all fall under masks becomes a hole showing the empty field, and one with an unmasked image is rebuilt from that alone. Masks belong to the observation, not to any plane: each has a position (an angle on the observed picture), a **Size** (the semi-major axis $r$), an axis ratio $q$, and a position angle $\varphi$, and covers $x_r^2 + (y_r/q)^2 \le r^2$ in the same rotated frame as the analytic sources (<a href="/caustica-documentation/source-models/">§6</a>). They are placed with the mask tool (key `4`, or **+ Mask** in the Observation panel), offered while there is an observation, by clicking the image in any view or the observation's card; the lens, source and hybrid tools place nothing on that card. The panel lists them, each with an eye to turn it off and a × to delete it, and a click on one selects it and opens it in the Object panel. Masks are drawn as gray circles in every view except line art, and on the observation's card, so one can be selected and edited from anywhere; in the lensed-image view and on the card the outline and a light fill also show the patch it covers, which is the place to line one up against the lens galaxy. Only the delensed view is affected by them. They are tested per pixel at the fragment's image-plane position in the mesh pass, so the cut is exact whatever the mesh resolution, and up to 16 are applied (the panel says if more are on). Like any object they can be hidden, dragged, copied, undone, animated, and saved in a config, as entries of the top-level `masks:` list (`cx`, `cy`, `r`, `q`, `phi`, `hidden`).

<div class="doc-warning" markdown="1">
**Heads up.** Light from the lens galaxy itself is not removed unless a mask covers it: it is traced back like everything else and spread over the source plane, mostly around the radial caustic. The PSF is not undone either, so the reconstruction is as blurred as the observation, and differently so in each image, since each is magnified by a different amount.
</div>
